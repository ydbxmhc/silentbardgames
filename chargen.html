Characters

Building a Character

Mechanical elements serve the story, not the other way around. Your Thief isn't limited to "thief abilities" since there's no thief class. Your Knight doesn't have to fight with sword and shield just because you have swords and shields. You decide what your character's story is, then choose the pieces that best represent it.

Foundations: Level and Concept

Level in Level One is different from most systems in that it stands alone. Virtually everything else is subject to character concept. Your character's Level is the foundation everything else builds on. It represents something something, grit, and luck rolled into a single number — but it's not tied to what you do for a living. It does three crucial things:

  • Durability — it's literally your hit points, as we'll see later
  • Capability — attempt most things with just Level, though Roles improve your chances
  • Capacity — no Role can exceed your Level, so it defines your potential

Concept Drives Everything

Before you spend any Karma, get an idea who the character's story — What drives them? What would they risk their life for? You're translating character concept into game terms, not cataloguing every quirk and skill they possess. The game assumes competent people can handle routine tasks — it's the exceptional situations that require rolling.

The Power of Good Representation

It's a truth about games that veterans of other games might miss: Good narrative representation is more mechanically powerful than raw numbers. The mechanics reward thematic coherence!

Hooks — Your Character's Engine

If Roles define what they will do, Hooks define what they will do, and why. They're the motivations, the drives, and the complications that transform a collection of abilities into a character with actual agency in the game world. They're not just background; they have actual mechanical weight.

A good Hook should do three things:

They Drive the Story Forward. Hooks give your character reasons to take risks, make hard choices, and get involved in adventures. They're the answer to "why does my character care about this?" The GM also gets to use them against the party and your Hook is the engine that drives the story forward.

They Provide Mechanical Bonuses. Once per scene, it can be invoked to add its rank to any roll it's relevant. The GM may also apply it to rolls he thinks it should improve, even without consultation.

They Generate Karma! When Hooks complicate life, you get paid. The more dramatically a Hook impacts your choices, the more it's worth.

How They Work in Play

Say your character has a Hook 2 of Protect the Innocent! Dealing with a corrupt baron, you could:

  • Add its rank of 2 to one intimidation roll over their exploitation of workers
  • Earn Karma when you refuse a bribe because it would betray those you protect
  • Get an automatic +2 on any roll to deal with them as an NPC (GM's call)

Hooks make you better at the things your character cares most about, while creating the sort of complications that drive the stories you want to tell.

Crafting Good Hooks

They should be Disruptive, Not Dormant. Good Hooks don't wait politely to be relevant — they actively complicate your plans. "Cursed Bloodline" manifests at the most inconvenient moments. "Curiosity Killed the Cat" makes you investigate things you should leave alone. Being a "Pixie" compels mischief even when stealth would be smarter and easier…

They should Create Dilemmas. The best Hooks force difficult choices between things the character values. "Always Tell the Truth" is a problem when honesty conflicts with duty, friendship, mission goals, or survival. Being a "Pixie" means common folk will try to catch you when they shouldn't!

Hooks can be Player-Driven! You should invoke your own Hooks for Karma and Luck Tokens when it makes sense. The GM may also apply it occasionally — but "It's what my character would do" choosing to complicate your own plans sometimes it's exactly how Hooks should work. Just remember to check with the party and the other players so they don't kill you out of irritation.

Self-Hooking and Table Courtesy

One of the most powerful aspects of Hooks is that you can invoke them. When your Paladin character could easily stay quiet and safe, but you are sworn to chivalric ideals, it's glorious to stand and say "Here! I am here!" It's going to be a problem, but everyone knows Sir Gael'fric is that guy and they respect him for it. This creates a beautiful partnership between player and character motivation.

However — and this is crucial — self-hooking comes with real-world social responsibility. Choosing to pickpocket someone while the party sneaks to earn some cash and Karma? Fine, as long as it's handled gracefully. That's not Hook pursuit, that's spotlight hogging of the rudest sort. The GM should never let you get away with it, but you should never put them in that position.

Self-hooking should create complications that involve the whole table; they don't exclude them. The Pixie causing mischievous infiltration affects everyone's plans. The knight charging obviously cursed artifact drags the whole party into consequences. Everyone should get a chance to say "Oh, crap!" and join in. The goal is more interesting stories for everyone, not just more screen time for yourself.

Common Pitfalls

Be Good doesn't create complications, just mostly advantages. Lucky in Love doesn't create complications, just mostly advantages. Alcoholic has very limited helpful applications.

You want something that will cause you trouble, but you also need to clearly imagine how it helps. Will it make me better at things? Will it come it too often? Often enough to be interesting? Not so much of a problem the group gets tired of it being invoked?

These questions make the mechanical choices for Roles and Maneuvers easier because you get a clearer picture of the kinds of problems your character frequently faces and how they work through them.

Hooks and Growth

Hooks are triggered during a session, you may rank it up at the end of the session. When a Hook is triggered during a session, you may rank it up at the end of the session. You're not required to — maybe you'd rather keep it at the current rank. The GM might suggest changing the Hook if it's not providing enough complications. If you consistently avoid invoking it, consider describing it differently, making it broader, or replacing it.

When the GM proposes a Hook event and you decline, you must immediately pay a Luck Token or refuse to keep the Hook altogether. You don't have to take the complication, and you aren't being punished for refusing, but declining a Hook is supposed to be costly. You have to be willing to let it cause real trouble or you shouldn't have it.

Any time a Hook changes value (can or could) have: you may change its description at session's end. The GM might also suggest changing the Hook after an interesting growth experience. If you increase it, it might be worth describing it differently. The "Once Bitten, Twice Shy" might become "Painfully Cautious" as your character learns from repeatedly touching hot stoves. The more dramatically it defines your character, the more mechanics it has.

This creates a living system where your character's motivations become stronger and more nuanced over time as they grow.
