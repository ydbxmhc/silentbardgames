Running the Game

Wealth Management

Character wealth is a common problem in games. Being poor can be inconvenient for the characters and frustrating for the players; being "too rich" on the other hand can pose issues. How do you manage PC wealth?

Level-Based Wealth Abstraction

Generally, currencies are the province of individual settings. If you want to bypass all of that you can just make every purchase of any significance a roll. Apply losses as a significant Hindrance on the character's liquidity; it won't affect them for other rolls, but they lose purchasing power until they address the Hindrance in the fiction.

This will generally mean making money by hiring out for jobs or looting treasure. They can also make rolls against their current Level to earn money through their skills, the charge lower prices for them. Walking into a saloon and having the bartender cheerfully quip "Your money's no good here, mate!" while pouring you a tall lager is sometimes as good or better than actual coin.

This implicitly links their purchasing power to their Level, which isn't entirely crazy, but has some side effects that may seem odd. A Level 3 PC with no Role at all won't have the purchasing power of a same-Level Performer with haggling-relevant Maneuvers of Armor 1. It makes a certain amount of sense that a character with more worldliness and reputation might attract more wealth.

Also, always remember that you shouldn't bother to roll for things that aren't really consequential and interesting. New characters may struggle to afford a beer, but well-established heroes wouldn't bother. If they have a Big Spender Hook, maybe they find out when they try to pay that they don't have enough after all.

You never have to do it this way, though.

Barter

Well-established and "universally valued" currencies of grand empires are historically tools of rich urban areas. Out in the fringes where characters often start there will be towns and villages, but they deal often as not in direct trade of goods and services for goods and services in return. How many eggs you get for a rabbit skin depends on the quality of the skin, and how bad the chicken farmer needs it, and how many eggs he has… This is ideal for setting up simple quests. Where larger characters would be expected to barter for more rabbit skins for 3 more basilisk venom to get the most supple leather; and be offered Silent Boots in trade.

Don't fall into the modern mindset that any price is fixed. Merchants charge what they can, and everyone haggles. Your gold florins are good in Forenza, but a merchant in Gerchdall will eye them with suspicion, bite them, weigh them, and offer less than he really thinks they are worth. Don't use Uneedy.* Your silk vaults will often be portable tools of wealth — things like bullion and artworks, and real wealth is land.

Traditional Currency Tracking

There's nothing wrong with counting coin if that's your preference. Currency standards were invented for a reason, and cities, guilds, and trading companies all work together to maintain their consistency and validity. Measured monetary units are extremely useful in cities, and if your game is going to be low-wealth for the foreseeable future then coins are a great treasure for characters to find. Just remember that prices change.

Don't fall into the trap of making every shopkeep sell the same inventory for the same prices — come up with three "shop prices" and have it shift around. It's fine to track details, but one sure way to tell a medieval-style fantasy setting is going to bore home the inconsistencies.

There should be things you just don't "just buy" because money alone isn't enough. You want shops with powerful magic swords on racks with pricelags, we're not here to tell you magic swords can't be found — but we are here to tell you magic there should be a story, a quest, and a cost beside just changing the number in the character's bank ledger.

* A palm-sized 2in/5cm cube of pure gold is over five and a half pounds/2500g.
