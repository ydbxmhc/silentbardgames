Gear

# Creating Items

Most gear is bought, scavenged, or handed to you by someone who wanted a favor.
Some gear you *make*. A masterwork blade, a suit of powered armor, a spirit
bound into a length of linen - these are projects, and projects take time,
skill, and a willingness to have the work beat you senseless.

Other things you make in a hurry, on the spot, because you need them now. A
charm against the thing at the door. A bottle of something unpleasant with a
rag stuffed in the neck.

Both are covered here. They work differently.

---

# Lasting Work

Creating a lasting item is a single [opposed
action](resistance-and-opposition.md) that plays out across downtime. It is not
a series of small victories. It is one long fight with the thing you are trying
to make.

## The Rating Is A Budget

Before you begin, declare what you are building. The item's **rating** is the
size of the pot you get to spend on its [Traits](traits.md) - and it is also
the [EL](core-mechanics.md) the item opposes you with while you work.

Declare a rating 4 item and you are fighting an EL4 opposition for the right to
spend 4 points on Traits. Declare rating 8 and you are fighting EL8 for a
bigger pot. Ambition is not free; it is the difficulty.

You do not need a rank cap on this. The math already handles it. Reach past
what you can actually execute and the work will tell you so.

## Spending The Pot

Points come out of the pot on the **[Level](core-mechanics.md) progression** -
the first rank costs 1, the second costs 2, the third costs 3, and so on. Rated
and unrated [Traits](gear-traits.md) draw from the same pot and the same
progression.

A rating 4 item might be:

- One rated [Trait](gear-traits.md) at rank 2, plus one unrated (2 + 1, with 1
  left)
- Two unrated [Traits](gear-traits.md), plus one rank of a rated one
- A single rated [Trait](gear-traits.md) at rank 3 (1 + 2), with a point spare

*Sharp 1* and *Sturdy* together is a rating 2 item. That is not magic. That is
a good smith who took his time and knew what he was doing. Call it Dwarven work
if you like the flavor.

an already-Traited item - you must fold in the existing complexity but cannot
spend those points. Under consideration: letting an artificer with previously
crafted items on hand total their spare points and roll them collectively once
per new work as a [Boost](actions-and-timing.md#boost-actions). Neat, but it

## Doing The Work

- Work happens **only in downtime**, one roll per **session**.
- **No [Luck Tokens](luck.md).** Luck does not build things. Patience does.
- You *may* spend [Karma](karma.md) as though it were a Token. Downtime is when
  you are allowed to buy your way through a bad night at the bench.
- The item opposes every roll at its declared rating.

Because it is one long opposition with no [recovery](recovery.md) rolls between
attempts, whatever the work does to you *stays* on you until the project
resolves. Fatigue accumulates. So does everything else.

The only thing that improves in your favor is you. Your ranks can go up between
sessions, and a project that was beating you in the spring may be manageable by
autumn.

Note that the opposition never gets easier. A rating 4 item pushes back at EL4
on the last session as hard as it did on the first. A crafter who is barely
qualified faces a long grind of coin-flip exchanges, accumulating Harm the
whole way. A crafter well above the rating finishes quickly and cleanly. Same
rule, and it gives you "the master takes a week, the apprentice takes a year
and looks terrible afterward" without a word of extra text.

## Three Ways It Ends

**You finish.** The item exists, with the [Traits](gear-traits.md) you bought.
It is yours, it is permanent, and it does not degrade with ordinary use the way
transient gear does.

**You are taken [Down](feeling-down.md).** The project fails. Time and
materials are gone, the work is ruined, and you have to recover from having
your skull handed to you by an object. This is what overreach costs.

**You cede.** Give up before it finishes. You lose everything you invested -
time, materials, the whole run - but you back out before doing permanent harm.
The unfinished work simply is not.

Ceding is always available and always feels like cowardice. That is the point.
It is the smart play far more often than pride will let you take it.

## Improving An Existing Item

You can come back to something you already made. This is the only way partial
progress survives: you do not build half a sword, you build a *finished* sword
and then attempt to make it more.

**All [Traits](gear-traits.md) accumulate into the item's rating.** So the
masterwork blade that is already *Sharp 1, Sturdy* is a rating 2 item - and
attempting to add *Flaming 2* means declaring the new total and opposing the
higher [EL](core-mechanics.md) for the whole attempt.

The good sword resists becoming a great sword. Each enchantment is a harder
fight than the last, against a thing you care about more every time.

And the failure state has not changed. Lose, and **the item is destroyed in the
process** - not just the new work, the sword. Cede, and you keep the blade but
lose everything you spent on the attempt.

This is why there are so few legendary weapons. Most of them were destroyed by
someone who wanted one more Trait.

# Pinned Work

Disposables are different. A potion, a charm, a scroll, a trap, a bottle of
burning oil - these are made in the moment, they are used once, and they are
gone. They do not need downtime and they do not fight you.

They need a [Luck Token](luck.md).

## The Mechanic

Make an appropriate skill roll as a [Wager](wagered-actions.md), on the
[Residual Effect](wagered-actions.md) model, then **pin it by allocating a
[Luck Token](luck.md)**.

The Token is not *spent*. It is **encumbered** - it does not refresh while the
pinned effect exists. You may pin as many effects as you have Tokens to
allocate, and you will feel every one of them.

- **You commit on the attempt, not the result.** Roll badly and you have a
  mediocre charm occupying a Token. Dismissing it loses the Token anyway. You
  may try again when a fresh one comes around, but more of the same is not
  another scene.
- **Anything that ends the fetish spends the Token** - use, accident, or
  someone deliberately breaking it. It then comes back at the normal rate: one
  per scene, up to your Level.
- Allocation reduces the pool you have available, not your ceiling.

A [Spellweaver](roles/spellweaver.md) with more spells than he cares to bind
can store one in a fetish this way. So can anyone else with a relevant skill
and something to make it out of.

## Pinned Effects Do Not Roll

**This is the important part.** A pinned effect is fixed at creation. It is the
whole effect. It does not add dice, it does not roll, it just *is*.

The test is the same one the [Assist Rule](assist-rule.md) uses: **does it make
decisions?**

A potion, a scroll, a charm, a trap - none of these decide anything. They
deliver exactly what was banked, every time, without variance. A summoned
spirit *does* decide, even when you gave it careful instructions in advance,
because it still has to interpret them. So the spirit rolls its own
[EL](core-mechanics.md) and belongs to the GM. The potion does not.

A *Potion of Strength 3* does not hand the drinker three dice to roll. It gives
him three points of Strength that are simply there, part of his
[EL](core-mechanics.md) for anything Strength-based. He rolls his own actions
as he always would, with a bigger number.

A pinned *Persistent Area Fax Iacta* fills the room with 3 points of effect
every exchange. Toss it and run. No rolls.

Reliability is worth a great deal in a game made of dice, and the Token is what
you pay for it. A caster who wants certainty pins it. A caster who wants
flexibility keeps the Token and takes his chances like everyone else.

## Trigger, Effect, and Target

All three are declared when the item is made, by any method - alchemy,
enchantment, trapmaking, whatever fits. **The declaration is the item.**

- **Molotov** - the trigger is deliberately complicated: light it, throw it
  where you meant to, break the bottle. Each stage can fail on its own. Throw
  it at your own feet and you probably did it wrong.
- **Trap** - the trigger is stressing the tripwire. The target is the area the
  spike swings through. It fires on anyone, and it does not know you.
- **Flaming oil** - the target is the weapon you put it on; the trigger is
  lighting it. Inert in your pack until you want it. Also inert in your pack
  when somebody else decides to light it.
- **Potion of Strength** - the trigger is drinking it, the target is the
  drinker, and it had better also be *Persistent* or it fires during the single
  turn spent swallowing and is gone before he can use it.
- **Fetish of Strength** - operates on whoever is wearing it. The trigger is
  putting it on properly. Take it off and it loses its target, which ends it.
- **Something anyone can pick up and use, and pass around** - that is permanent
  enchantment. Go back to the top of this page.

Trigger complexity is a real design choice. "Breaks when thrown" is simple and
dependable. "Activates when the wearer's blood is spilled" is precise, safe,
and completely useless if the fight goes well.

## The Declaration Is The Item

A pinned item is not a container of magic waiting to be repurposed. It is a
**completed action waiting for its cue**, and the GM should hold that line.

Pour your *Potion of Strength* onto a cloth and polish your sword with it, and
here is what happens: you polish a sword. Use your [Tinker](roles/tinker.md)
skill, exactly as you would with cheap lamp oil, and enjoy the shine. The
potion is wasted, because *that was not the trigger*. Nothing was ever defined
to happen there, so nothing does.

This feels harsh the first time it comes up. Hold the line anyway. It is also
what makes a world full of charms interesting - most of them were made for one
situation and are good for nothing else, and stumbling across somebody's old
cache is a puzzle rather than a payday. What was this *for*?

## Who Pays

Anyone can supply the Token. **The only requirement is that they do it
knowingly and mean it.**

Go to the witch for a charm against the thing that comes at night and tries to
talk you into opening the door. She asks a great many questions, gathers herbs
and powders and a sliver of cold iron, and then insists that *you* cut yourself
on the iron and speak your intention aloud.

She is not putting her own Token in your pocket. That ritual is not
atmosphere - it is the payment.

- The charm can be for your child. A Token is a Token, so long as you
  understand what it costs. You will be one Token poorer for as long as she
  wears it, and nobody will ever see that but you.
- An artificer may absolutely supply the Token himself so that the Healer
  survives the next fight. Entirely valid. He simply plays at reduced luck for
  as long as the charm lasts, on purpose, for someone else.
- If the whole party wants charms, the whole party brings Tokens. Capability
  does not pool up in the maker.

This is the same principle as [the Accords](magick/conjuring.md): anyone may
pay your debts, provided they do it *on purpose*. The question is never who
paid. It is whether the payment was deliberate.

Which makes this hard to abuse, though not impossible. Nobody can pin your
Token by sleight of hand - you have to sacrifice it willingly, for a stated
purpose. A con has to misdirect you about the *purpose* instead: the ward you
gladly paid into, which protects the village exactly as promised, while the
trigger and target you were not paying attention to do something else entirely.
You meant it. You were simply wrong about what you meant it for.

That is a far more sinister thing than a trick question, and a much better
story.
