Core Mechanics

Harm & Recovery

Where Are My Hit Points???

Take a deep breath… There aren't any.

Yes, this means anyone can be taken out of the action in a single hit — but so can you. It is this way intentionally — heroes grow into legendary toughness, but the system is always as dangerous as the GM wants to make it. Harm is a modifier that reduces your effectiveness on future rolls by counting directly against your base Level. When total Harm exceeds your Level, you're Down — at least temporarily.

This, yes, means anyone can be taken out easily at low levels. It's intentional — heroes grow into legendary toughness, but the system is always as dangerous as the GM wants to make it.

Three Types of Harm

All these types reduce EL, but Hindrances don't count toward going Down.

Hindrances can't be recovered at all. They must be dealt with through the story. They are situational conditions, not damage. Blindfolded? Tied up? The guard is extremely intimidated and will not let you pass? Hindrances. The only way to recover from them is to address the cause: cut the ropes, remove the blindfold, somehow calm the guard. They apply to relevant rolls, but because they don't cause Down they can stack up indefinitely without dropping the character.

Complications disappear completely on a successful recovery roll. Think short-term fatigue, vertigo from a head wound, or losing your nerve after seeing someone die. What? What are you saying? Complications can take you Out? Yes — if you have enough of them. This is by design; a hero who takes a lot of minor hits should eventually go down from accumulation even if none of them are serious, but recovery is quick and easy.

Injury is recovered per day with rest, more quickly with medical attention. Bruised ribs, sprained ankles, broken bones, lacerations — the real physical harm that takes time to heal.

A Clarification

It is important to understand that Harm does not mean damage. Harm is anything which impedes a character, or takes away their agency. Psychological Harm that results in ongoing loss of confidence can be as debilitating as a broken leg. Social Harm that affects a character's reputation can certainly make them less effective in many situations, though if the character doesn't care about such things, the GM should be careful to apply something that would matter to them. Most of these can and should be handled with the usual rules.

Feeling Down?

When Injury plus Complications exceeds your base Level, you're Down. This doesn't necessarily mean unconscious, but you can't act meaningfully in the scene any more. The only rolls you can make are passive resistance — no more charging into battle or casting spells until you recover enough to get up and about. If the GM allows any actions besides that, it must be Free Actions. Note that in some cases, Down merely indicates a loss of agency — the character might have been charmed, and forced to run away.

You stay Down until your total harm drops below your Level again.

Staggered

If that's too restrictive for your preferred style of play, keep going! You can keep taking turns until you simply have no positive EL left. At that point, you are definitely Down.

Until then, as long as you can keep making rolls with positive results the GM might let you keep struggling, but any time you completely fail to achieve any positive effect on two consecutive turns you have to make a resistance roll against your current Harm to stay conscious.

Yes, this means positive EL in ANY Role counts toward staying awake enough to at least call out advice to your teammates!

Recovery

Not all Harm is created equal. The difference isn't just in the numbers, but in how you get over it.

We've already said Hindrances require story solutions, not just dice. Dispel the blindness, cut the ropes, or somehow calm the guard.

Complications disappear completely on a successful recovery roll. Think of asking a friend or teammate; they might offer something more than a mechanical boost, and the GM might let you try. Think shorter-term fatigue, losing your nerve in the middle of a battle, vertigo — these really should just go away, and quickly.

Injury is really recovered once per day with rest, more quickly with a Healer. Roll your Recovery against the Injury itself. Success reduces the Injury by the amount of effect generated. The GM generates none — an Injury that gets no Treatment is just going to heal at the normal rate, and that roll just tells you how much faster it heals. GMs who want a more dangerous game can include active Complications; for a lighter game, don't.

The Oddball Rule

Recovery rolls don't quite follow the normal rules. You're always free to try. Free Action rolls can happen anytime they make sense, and even unconscious characters can have recovery rolls happen in the background. The key difference: you don't need to beat opposition — you just need to succeed. That's an unopposed roll!

Making It Dark

One of the great things about this system is you can tune injury vs complications for your group's preferred style of play. If you want a grittier, more historically accurate world, you can treat them as more or less dangerous than the default.

Second Wind

Players may say they want to take a minute to catch their breath during the fight. Even if this doesn't cost them a turn, it's still a recovery. A successful Recovery roll with no Healer or preparation lets them "get a grip" — more of the Harm comes back as Complications instead of Injury, and those recover quickly. No Recovery Mid-Fight without Magick!

The Messy Reality Of Not Knowing How Bad It Is

Most harm during a fight is a mix of real injury and temporary shock. You won't know how badly you're actually hurt until the adrenaline wears off.

Smart GMs call for a recovery roll soon after a conflict once characters have had a chance to breathe and bind their wounds, maybe eat or even sleep. Any harm that disappears was just Complications like temporary stunning and pain. Whatever remains is real Injury that needs time to heal. (Any actual Injury there from before can't be healed this way.)

This means it's better to describe Harm as "stunned and bleeding" rather than "gaping chest wound." Let the post-fight recovery roll reveal how much was just shock and how much was real.

This means the default is to treat it as all Injury until you know otherwise; for new damage, the default is to assume it's all Injury unless you have reason to include Harm penalties in this roll; for new damage, the default is to assume all Injury.

So… What Do I Roll?

Always include base Level in Recovery rolls! Not much else will count, but Boosts from Healers providing care, but those do not stack. Additional Healers may try to Boost their senior member so that their roll is better, but only one bonus may be applied for medical care (though other roles might usefully reduce relevant situational Hindrances.)

Recovery rolls are expected to include the penalties of accumulated Injury.

GMs who want a harsher game can include active Complications. For a lighter game, don't include Harm penalties in recovery rolls at all. Including Harm penalties in the roll to determine how much was Injury will make a game grimmer and deadlier, while leaving it out is more cinematic. If adding a second roll for armor, they don't have to use the same sets of modifiers…

Advanced Options

Simple Rolls: you can skip the resistance roll for wounds and just use the current wound score as modifier.

Magical Healing: Spells can heal Injury after effect, but you might prefer them to heal Injury point for point after resistance rather than just as points. This is harsh; effect is what matters.

Realistic Healing: Cap recovery at one point per day regardless of rolls. If you want to make healing precious, use it.

Lethality: By default, Down characters become unconscious or incapacitated until the threat ends, but don't die unless dramatically appropriate (yes, OSR tables can kill player characters and pets by agreeing they are dead.) However, a Down companion means lost bonuses, reduced tactical options, and genuine emotional stakes.

Critical Complications: A fail on a recovery is always bad. The wound has developed an additional problem, such as an infection, and the wound gets to make an attack roll. It goes to make an attack on a future roll. It goes to make the wound active; no treatment means the character must accept a relevant Hook or face permanently worsened conditions. To resolve them, the character must accept a relevant Hook.
